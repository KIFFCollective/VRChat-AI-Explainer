01 / THE MANDATE

95% agree. On what, exactly?

The headline finding is that 95% of respondents want a label for AI usage. It is also the least useful number in the entire post, and VRChat knows it, because two paragraphs later they publish the number that undoes it.

Give people eight example categories and ask them to build a labeling scheme, and you get 3,141 different schemes. The most popular one was chosen by fewer than 10% of respondents. That is not a mandate for a label. That is 21,595 people each imagining a different feature and agreeing only on the verb.

Read it the other way and it gets more interesting: a 95% supermajority that cannot produce a 10% plurality has agreed on a feeling, not a spec. The feeling is "I want to know what I am walking into." That is a legitimate demand. It is just not a design document.

The tell: the post treats 3,141 combinations as evidence the community is confused. It is evidence the community is specific. People know exactly which disclosures matter to them, and none of them agree on which eight.

Wanting to know is not wanting out.

Here is the number the post buries in the middle: only a third of respondents would deliberately avoid an "AI-assisted" world. So the thing 95% are asking for is information, not filtration.

That has a consequence VRChat should say out loud, and does not: a label would mostly be read, not acted on. Which makes their refusal to ship filters entirely consistent with their own data, and makes their refusal to ship a label look like an argument they lost to themselves.

The 37% free-text figure is the real centre of gravity. It is not "no AI" and it is not "anything goes." It is a test that almost nobody can administer: who made the creative decisions? That question is not in the eight categories, and it is the one that would actually settle this.

03 / ACTUAL CONSENSUS

The room agrees on quality, not provenance.

Three items cleared the "least divisive" bar in the survey, including among respondents who use AI. All three are about being lied to, not about who wrote the code.

VRChat built the plan on exactly this, and it is the best decision in the post: accurate thumbnails first, discovery that rewards quality second, in-game feedback third. They read the alignment correctly and moved on it. The follow-through, though, is where it gets thin, because they also admit they cannot detect a misleading thumbnail at scale.

Note the shape: the widest demand (95%) is the one with no agreed implementation. The narrowest (69%) ships first. That is not a coincidence, it is what consensus looks like.
04 / THE MISSING CONTEXT

Meanwhile, on Steam: record crowd, worst mood.

The post never mentions the platform's own vitals. So here they are. In the same window, the last 30 days of Steam reviews sit at 35% positive, against 70% across all 180,448 reviews ever left.

A platform at near-record concurrency with a 35% recent review rate is not a churn story. It is a mood story. Reviews are a mood ring: they measure who is angry enough to type, not who is still logging in.

Steam will not tell you why without reading them, and I could not read them (the review endpoint blocks automated requests), so I am not going to invent a cause. What the gap does establish is this: whichever way you read the AI fight, it has not cost VRChat its audience yet. It has cost it its shop window. "Yet" is the word that should worry a trust and safety team.

It also means the post's central claim, that no single group's answer should drive the plan, is arithmetically safe. The loudest group is not the biggest group. That is the one place where the survey and the Steam data agree, and the post should have said it with these numbers attached.

05 / THE REAL ARGUMENT

Enforcement is the whole argument, and it is one paragraph long.

Buried in the middle of the post is the strongest thing in it: "we have no way to automate detection of something being 'misleading,' so early enforcement has been challenging, and we would face the same enforcement challenge with any type of labeling."

That is correct, and it deserves to be the headline rather than a parenthetical. Every label VRChat could ship is self-declared. A self-declared label is worth exactly as much as the honesty of the people with the strongest incentive to lie about it, and the post says so itself: some creators would label honestly, others would pass off assisted work as hand-made.

The conclusion they draw is "so don't ship a label." The conclusion the argument actually supports is "ship a label that admits what it is." A "Human-authored" mark described as a claim, not a certification costs nothing to build and stops being a trap the moment it is not treated as a verdict. They are three quarters of the way to that position and have not said it.

Read the gap: the survey ran in October 2026. The answer on labels lands by end of Q1 2027, five months later, and the promise is "more news," not a decision. Meanwhile the things that ship are the things with consensus. That is defensible project management and it is also exactly how a hard question gets parked until it becomes someone else's quarter.
07 / WHAT TO WATCH

Four numbers that will settle this better than another survey.

The post ends by promising to keep listening. Fine. Here is what listening should look like in numbers, with the dates attached.

01
Does the like/dislike rate survive month one? A world rating is the most gameable thing VRChat will ever ship. Watch the top 20 worlds for coordinated swings.

The survey's best result is the one VRChat did not put in bold: a community that cannot agree on a label still agrees on not being deceived. That is a workable mandate. Everything else in the post is scheduling.
